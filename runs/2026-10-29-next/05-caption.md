Address poisoning: you checked the first 4 and last 4 characters, they matched, and the money still went to a scammer.

Here's how the crypto copy-paste scam works. A scammer's computer makes wallet addresses until one starts and ends like one you use. Then it sends you a tiny or zero-value transfer, so the lookalike lands in your own transaction history. Next time you copy "the usual" address from history, you copy theirs. Once it's sent, no bank can pull it back.

In May 2024, someone sent a small test payment, then about $68 million in wrapped bitcoin to a lookalike. It was returned six days later after a negotiation. That one campaign had 82,031 lookalike addresses.

How to send crypto safely: check the whole address, middle included. Never copy from your history or from random tiny transfers. Pay a saved contact, username or QR code instead.

Send this to someone who only checks the first and last few characters.

Sources: Chainalysis, "Anatomy of an Address Poisoning Scam" (chainalysis.com/blog/address-poisoning-scam) · MetaMask Help Center, "Address poisoning scams" (support.metamask.io) · TRM Labs, "Understanding address poisoning on the TRON blockchain" (trmlabs.com)
