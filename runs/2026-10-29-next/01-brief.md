# Brief — The address you copied was a scammer's

Pillar: 3. Safe transacting (scams, address poisoning, how to check a transaction) | DYNK feature shown: the DYNK send screen paying a username / contact / QR code instead of a pasted address, in the background only, not as a pitch | Audience: non-crypto people and new holders who send crypto by copying an address from their recent transactions or an old message. Also anyone who has ever checked "the first few and last few characters" and called it done.

One takeaway (≤15 words): Never copy an address from your history; check the whole thing, every time.

Misconception we correct: "If the first and last few characters match, it's the right address" and "everything in my own transaction history is safe because it's my wallet." In address poisoning, a scammer makes an address that starts and ends the same as one you use, then puts it in your history. The address looks familiar, so you copy it.

General crypto facts (correct, keep general):
- A wallet address is a long string of letters and numbers. Most people only check a few characters at the start and end, and many apps shorten addresses to just those.
- Attackers can generate addresses whose first and last characters match an address you use.
- They then send a tiny or zero-value transfer (or a fake token) to or from you, so the lookalike address appears in your transaction history.
- Next time you copy "your usual" address from history, you may copy theirs.
- Blockchain transfers can't be reversed by a bank or the app; once sent to the wrong address, the funds are generally gone.
- Defences: check the full address (or many characters in the middle, too), use saved contacts / usernames / QR codes, never copy from transaction history, send a small test first, and ignore unexpected tiny incoming transfers.
